# Speaker Script — *Climate Shocks and the Financial Health of American Households and Hospitals*

Companion to `committee_presentation_20260615_hospital.tex` (27 numbered slides).
Slide numbers below match the footer page number in the bottom-right of each slide.
Target length **≈ 22–25 minutes**. Italics in *[brackets]* are stage directions, not spoken.

**One-sentence thesis to keep in your head:** *climate shocks raise the financial
burden of health — and that burden shows up on two ledgers, households (premiums and
medical debt) and the hospitals that serve them (uncompensated care and margins) —
with the same scarring and the same concentration in the most vulnerable.*

---

## Opening

**Title slide** *(≈30 s)*
> "Thank you all for being here. My dissertation asks a deceptively simple question:
> when a county gets hit by a climate shock — an extreme drought, a brutally hot or
> cold year, a bad air-quality year — who ends up paying for it, financially, through
> the health system? The answer has two sides: American **households**, and the
> **hospitals** that serve them. I'll show you three papers, and for each one, both
> sides of that ledger."

---

## Setup (slides 1–8) — *≈5 min*

**Slide 1 — Overview** *(≈45 s)*
> "Three papers, one U.S. panel — about 3,155 counties and 51 states over 2011 to 2023.
> Paper 1, **Incidence**: do shocks raise the financial burden of health? Paper 2,
> **Persistence**: do those costs reverse, scar, or compound? Paper 3, **Inequality**:
> does structural vulnerability amplify them? The empirical backbone is two-way fixed
> effects with distributed lags, local projections for dynamics, and natural-experiment
> and Callaway–Sant'Anna difference-in-differences against never-exposed counties.
> Everything is estimated at both state and county level."

**Slide 2 — Three Papers (table)** *(≈40 s)*
> "Here's the whole dissertation on one slide. Incidence asks *how much*, Persistence
> asks *how long*, Inequality asks *who bears it*. The headline of each: cold and
> drought raise medical debt and premiums through a household income channel; drought
> debt *scars* while cold employment *compounds*; and the harm is amplified where
> structural vulnerability is highest. Each row will get a household result and — new
> in this version — a hospital result."

**Slide 3 — Econometric Robustness** *(≈40 s)*
> "Before the findings, the methods additions that close prior committee feedback:
> random-effects was tested and rejected, the headlines survive; we added never-exposed
> DiD controls; humidity is now controlled; and we mirror every estimate across state
> and county aggregation. I'll return to a couple of these at the end."

**Slide 4 — [Transition] Conceptual Model and Hypotheses** *(≈10 s — segue)*
> "So let me lay out the mechanism we think is operating, and what the literature
> predicts."

**Slide 5 — Conceptual Model** *(≈45 s)*
> "The chain is: a climate shock hits a county, it works through household income and
> health-care utilization, and it surfaces as a financial outcome — medical debt,
> premiums, hospital bad debt, spending. The key modeling choice is that the shock is
> *recurring*, not absorbing: counties enter and exit shock states year to year, which
> is why we use impulse-response and onset/exit designs rather than a single event date."

**Slide 6 — Mechanism Pathways** *(≈45 s)*
> "Four pathways, each anchored to the literature: heat delays care; cold shifts
> utilization toward acute and emergency care; drought runs through agricultural income
> to debt; and air quality drives respiratory and cardiac costs. The drought-to-income-
> to-debt pathway has the strongest convergence — Hornbeck, Burke–Hsiang–Miguel,
> Carleton, and our own 2012 drought experiment all point the same way at one-to-three-
> year horizons."

**Slide 7 — Hypotheses** *(≈40 s)*
> "What the literature predicts versus what we find: drought lag-2 and cold lag-1 raising
> debt — as predicted. Long-run cold *compounding* — stronger than predicted. And
> humidity raising debt — no prior literature, a genuinely new result. So we confirm
> some priors and surprise on others."

## Paper 1 — Incidence (slides 9–13) — *≈4 min*

**Slide 8 — [Transition] Paper 1: Deferred Costs** *(≈10 s — segue)*
> "Paper 1. Do climate shocks raise the financial burden of health — on households and
> hospitals — with a lag?"

**Slide 9 — Paper 1: Prior Evidence from the Panel** *(≈45 s)*
> "From the panel fixed-effects models: cold shocks at a one-year lag and drought at a
> two-year lag both raise medical-debt share and benchmark premiums. The transmission
> runs through household income, not through systemic health spending — which is exactly
> the demand-side story. These are the level effects that motivate the natural experiment."

**Slide 10 — The 2012 Midwest Drought** *(≈40 s)*
> "To get cleaner identification we use the 2012 Midwest drought as a natural experiment —
> a sharp, well-defined shock with a large never-exposed control group. This sets up a
> two-by-two and a Callaway–Sant'Anna design against counties that never experienced the
> shock over the whole window."

**Slide 11 — Paper 1: What the Natural Experiment Adds** *(≈45 s)*
> "The 2012 drought DiD: per-capita personal income falls about \$1,300 and civilian
> employment falls about 2,000 in treated counties, both strongly significant against
> never-exposed controls. The Callaway–Sant'Anna event-time profiles agree. So the
> level correlations hold up under a credible counterfactual — climate shocks really do
> impose a household financial cost."

**Slide 12 — Paper 1, Supply Side: Unpaid Bills Onto Hospitals** *(NEW — ≈60 s)*
> "Now the other ledger. I built a hospital-by-year panel from the NASHP Hospital Cost
> Tool — about 60,000 hospital-years, 5,100 hospitals — and attached each hospital's
> county climate shocks. *[point to table]* The same shocks that raise household debt
> raise hospital **uncompensated care**: cold years add about \$1.55 million per hospital,
> heat raises uncompensated care as a share of revenue. Importantly, average operating
> and net margins **don't** move — hospitals absorb the hit through the uncompensated-care
> line. The one negative number, drought, is a measurement caveat I'll flag again in
> Paper 3: drought changes patient mix and volume, so it moves the *measured* ratio
> without implying relief. The takeaway: this is the provider mirror of the household
> debt channel."

## Paper 2 — Persistence (slides 14–18) — *≈4 min*

**Slide 13 — [Transition] Paper 2: Scarring and Compounding** *(≈10 s — segue)*
> "Paper 2. Once a shock hits, do the costs reverse, scar, or compound?"

**Slide 14 — Paper 2: From Levels to Dynamics** *(≈40 s)*
> "Levels tell us a shock raises costs; they don't tell us what happens *after*. So we
> move to dynamics — post-exit local projections that trace the outcome for several years
> after a county leaves a shock state. That's what separates a one-time cost from a scar."

**Slide 15 — Paper 2: Two Designs for Persistence** *(≈45 s)*
> "Two complementary designs. First, **onset / persist / exit symmetry**: we test whether
> the cost of entering a shock is exactly undone on leaving — if not, that's hysteresis,
> a scar. Second, **cumulative dose**: a running count of shock-years per unit, to see
> whether repeated exposure compounds. Both reuse the same machinery on the household and
> hospital sides."

**Slide 16 — Paper 2: Three Hazards, Three Persistence Profiles** *(≈50 s)*
> "And we get three distinct profiles. **Drought debt scars** — it peaks around two years
> after exit and doesn't fully recover. **Cold employment compounds** — repeated cold
> exposure accumulates into larger labor-market losses. **Heat saturates** — the marginal
> effect flattens with more exposure. So 'persistence' isn't one thing; it depends on the
> hazard."

**Slide 17 — Paper 2, Supply Side: Drought Scars Hospital Balance Sheets** *(NEW — ≈55 s)*
> "The same scarring appears on hospital finances. Using the identical onset/exit symmetry
> test, **drought is asymmetric** for both uncompensated care and operating margin —
> entering and leaving a drought state don't cancel out, the p-values are 0.012 and 0.006.
> Cold and heat, by contrast, are symmetric — reversible — just like the household side.
> One honest nuance: the cumulative-dose effect on margins is mildly *positive*, which I
> read as survivorship and adaptation in an unbalanced panel, not protection. So the
> supply-side persistence story is **drought hysteresis**, not dose-driven decline."

## Paper 3 — Inequality (slides 19–24) — *≈5 min*

**Slide 18 — [Transition] Paper 3: Unequal Weather** *(≈10 s — segue)*
> "Paper 3. Averages hide distribution — who actually bears the cost?"

**Slide 19 — Paper 3: From Average Effects to Their Distribution** *(≈40 s)*
> "Papers 1 and 2 estimate population averages. But a given shock can be far more costly
> where households or providers are already fragile. So Paper 3 interacts every shock with
> a measure of structural vulnerability and asks where the harm concentrates."

**Slide 20 — Paper 3: Hazard × Exposure × Vulnerability Index** *(≈45 s)*
> "We build the vulnerability axis from the CDC Social Vulnerability Index, and combine it
> with hazard intensity and exposure into a single index. The interaction tells us the
> marginal effect of a shock at low versus high vulnerability — the slope of inequality,
> not just the average."

**Slide 21 — Paper 3: Structural Vulnerability Amplifies Climate Harm** *(≈55 s)*
> "And it does amplify. In high-SVI counties, heat *costs* jobs where in low-SVI counties
> it doesn't; cold's income hit is roughly eight times larger; drought pushes premiums up
> rather than down. The composite heat index lowers median income by about \$435 per
> standard deviation, highly significant. The real-economy outcomes — income, employment,
> premiums — carry this result cleanly."

**Slide 22 — Paper 3, Supply Side: Strain Concentrates in Safety-Net Hospitals** *(NEW — ≈55 s)*
> "And there's a provider analogue. Where high-SVI *counties* bore more household harm,
> high-Medicaid **safety-net hospitals** bear more heat-driven uncompensated care — the
> interaction is significant at p = 0.02. *[point to plot]* I want to be honest that this
> amplification is shock- and outcome-specific, not blanket: the cold and Medicaid-
> expansion interactions are strong but point in directions that need interpretation —
> the expansion-state result is genuinely surprising. But the cleanest finding mirrors the
> demand side: the providers least able to absorb a shock are the ones it hits hardest."

**Slide 23 — Credit-Bureau Medical Debt: Measurement Issues** *(≈50 s)*
> "One critical caveat, and I want to raise it before you do. The medical-debt outcome is
> *measurement-fragile*. The debt response concentrates in *less*-vulnerable counties, and
> the sign even reverses between county and state aggregation. Why? Credit-bureau debt
> requires insurance, a billed encounter, and a credit file — so higher-uninsurance areas
> record *less* measured debt. That's a recording artifact, not absence of harm. So I lead
> the inequality story with income, employment, and premiums, which replicate across
> aggregation; the debt outcome is reported *with* this caveat. The same caveat is why the
> hospital drought number went negative two papers ago."

## Robustness, summary, close (slides 24–27) — *≈3 min*

**Slide 24 — Humidity Does Not Overturn the Findings** *(≈45 s)*
> "A discussant might ask whether the cold-lag result is just humidity confounding. So we
> added PRISM mean dew point as a control, at both state and county level, on a constant
> sample. The cold-to-debt coefficient is stable to three significant figures — 0.0136 to
> 0.0137. Humidity itself turns out to predict higher medical debt, which is a new finding,
> but it does *not* drive the temperature or drought results."

**Slide 25 — Summary** *(≈50 s)*
> "To pull it together. **Incidence**: cold and drought raise medical debt and premiums
> via household income. **Persistence**: drought debt scars, cold employment compounds,
> heat saturates. **Inequality**: harm is amplified where vulnerability is highest. And
> the **supply side** now runs through all three — temperature shocks raise hospital
> uncompensated care, drought scars hospital margins, and heat strain concentrates in
> safety-net hospitals. Open issues I'm transparent about: credit-bureau debt is
> measurement-sensitive, the cumulative-CDD DiD carries a regional confound, and the
> headline cells survive Bonferroni correction within shock."

**Slide 26 — Thank you / Discussion** *(≈15 s)*
> "That's the project — two ledgers, one consistent story. I'd welcome your questions."

## Anticipated Q&A — quick-reference

- **"Why hospital *location* county, not patient residence?"** Provider heterogeneity
  (ownership, safety-net status, system) is a hospital attribute; we need the hospital as
  the unit, so each hospital inherits its own county's shocks (98.4% zip→county match).
  The residential split is still used for the consumer-side county debt measure.
- **"Is the positive margin dose-response real?"** Likely survivorship — hospitals that
  accumulate many shock-years and stay in the panel are the sturdier ones. We frame it as
  adaptation/survivorship, not causal protection.
- **"Why does drought *lower* measured uncompensated care?"** Same measurement fragility as
  credit-bureau debt — drought shifts patient mix, volume, and revenue, moving the measured
  ratio without implying financial relief. We lead with the temperature channel.
- **"Why do Medicaid-expansion states show *more* weather-elastic uncompensated care?"**
  Candidate reading: in expansion states the residual uninsured/bad-debt population is
  smaller but more sharply exposed to utilization swings; in non-expansion states
  uncompensated care is already structurally high and less shock-elastic. Reported as a
  discussion point, not a clean vulnerability story.
- **"Clustering?"** State-level throughout (nests rating areas and counties); premium
  models also reported with rating-area clusters.
