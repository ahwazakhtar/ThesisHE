# 6 Mechanisms

Cold and drought raise household financial distress at a one- to two-year lag. Across a U.S. county panel, a cold shock raises the share of residents carrying medical debt by about 1.1 percentage points one year later, extreme drought raises it by roughly 0.7 points after two years and pushes real annual employee premiums up by about $18, and the 2012 drought lowered per-capita income by roughly $1,300 and employment by about 2,000 jobs in exposed counties relative to never-exposed controls. Systemic per-capita health spending, by contrast, follows income and unemployment rather than the weather. These reduced-form relationships do not, on their own, identify the channel through which climate stress becomes financial stress. A leading candidate is agricultural income: climate shocks depress farm earnings, and lost income later surfaces as debt and forgone coverage (Deschênes and Greenstone, 2007). This section asks how much of the relationship operates outside that channel. The design bounds the agricultural channel rather than decomposing the effect into channel shares; what it establishes is that agriculture cannot be the whole story. A substantial part of the relationship operates outside farm income: the health-cost response is measured directly in Medicare data and requires no farm-income intermediary, the employment response loads on climate-exposed non-farm industries, and the distributional amplification operates through energy burden as well as farming.

The four channels examined below are not arbitrary. Each is a distinct pathway the climate–economy literature has identified between a weather shock and household financial distress, and each can be measured in data that do not pass through farm income, so together they can adjudicate the agricultural hypothesis. Two operate on the demand side of the health system: a direct morbidity-and-utilization channel, in which heat, cold, and poor air quality raise illness and medical spending (Deryugina et al., 2019), and a labor-exposure channel, in which the same shocks lower earnings through reduced productivity and hours (Graff Zivin and Neidell, 2014; Somanathan et al., 2021). The third, energy burden, is a distributional-affordability margin, in which shocks raise utility costs and crowd out other necessities for low-income households (Doremus et al., 2022; Barreca et al., 2016). The fourth moves to the supply side, asking whether the same shocks strain the hospitals that provide care. Migration enters separately, not as a cost channel but as an adjustment margin that can bias the persistence estimates.

## 6.1 Data and design

Estimation is on a U.S. county panel covering 2011–2023, linked to the auxiliary data sources introduced below. Climate shocks are defined relative to each county's own long-run distribution: the binary heat and cold indicators mark the top quartile of that county's historical degree-day distribution, and continuous measures are z-scores standardized to a frozen 1990–2000 baseline that precedes the study window. Every specification includes county and year fixed effects and enters the contemporaneous shock together with two annual lags, with standard errors clustered by state; the persistence specifications replace the on/off indicator with a cumulative count of shock-years, and a compound indicator captures being hit by at least one hazard. County fixed effects absorb secular trends and year fixed effects absorb nationally common shocks, so identification rests on idiosyncratic, county-specific tail realizations of the weather.

The strategy for separating the agricultural channel from the rest is to hold agricultural dependence fixed as a structural, pre-treatment county attribute rather than as contemporaneous farm income, which would sit on the causal path and constitute a bad control. I measure it two ways: the USDA ERS 2015 farming-dependent typology and a baseline (2001–2010) farm-earnings share. For each outcome and shock I then interact the shock with agricultural dependence, to test whether the effect loads on farm counties, and re-estimate within the bottom tercile of agricultural dependence — urban and service counties, where by construction any surviving effect does not operate through cropland. The identifying logic is that if agriculture were the whole story, the effect would disappear once the farm intermediary is removed. It does not.

## 6.2 A directly measured morbidity and utilization channel

The clearest evidence that the health-cost response operates outside agriculture comes from measuring medical cost and utilization directly. County Medicare records (CMS Geographic Variation, 2014–2023) carry no farm-income intermediary. Extreme heat raises standardized Medicare spending by $112 per beneficiary contemporaneously, $177 at a one-year lag, and $75 at two years (all *p* < 0.02), and raises emergency-department visits by 7.8 and 9.5 per 1,000 at the contemporaneous and one-year horizons (*p* = 0.006, 0.0002). Extreme cold raises both at a two-year lag ($85 in spending, 9.0 ED visits per 1,000; *p* = 0.009, 0.002), and poor air quality raises ED visits by 4.8, 3.3, and 2.8 per 1,000 across three lags, reproducing Deryugina et al. (2019) within the panel. None of these responses can run through farm income; they are a morbidity channel measured in administrative health data, and they would operate in any county with a hospital.

Two features of these records deserve emphasis. First, they are a *sentinel* population: Medicare covers the 65-and-over, so the estimates identify where the climate morbidity shock is cleanest to measure in administrative data, not the population in which household medical debt accrues — the working-age un- and under-insured. The claim is that shocks raise morbidity and utilization where measurement is clean, and that the same physiological insult plausibly reaches the uninsured working-age population where it surfaces as debt rather than as a billed Medicare claim; it is not that Medicare spending itself produces the debt. Second, the channels line up on timing once their measurement calendars are taken into account. Cold raises the medical-debt share at a one-year lag but Medicare spending at two, which looks inconsistent until one notes that a winter-*t* cold shock generates bills reaching the following August credit-bureau snapshot (*t*+1) within the standard 90-to-180-day collections pipeline, whereas annual Medicare totals smooth within-year timing and the two-year spending response reflects delayed sequelae. The lag structure by channel is therefore a consequence of how each outcome is recorded, not a contradiction.

## 6.3 Broad labor exposure

Where the climate effect resembles employment, it operates through broad labor exposure rather than cropland, and the cleanest evidence is where the damage concentrates across industries. The heat–employment response is more negative in counties with a larger share of employment in climate-exposed non-farm industries — construction, mining, manufacturing, transportation, and utilities: in log employment, a one-standard-deviation-higher exposed-industry share is associated with a roughly half-percent deeper heat effect (interaction −0.0052, *p* = 0.006), and the same pattern holds against energy burden (−0.0084, *p* = 0.005). Both survive replacing year fixed effects with division-by-year fixed effects (−0.0042 and −0.0078, *p* = 0.015 and 0.002), so neither is an artifact of the differential regional warming that a frozen climatology could otherwise smuggle into a heat coefficient. This is the labor-productivity mechanism of Graff Zivin and Neidell (2014) and Somanathan et al. (2021), which the agricultural story does not predict.

The cold–employment comparison across agricultural terciles requires more caution. In raw job counts it appeared to strengthen in the least-agricultural counties (a loss of 2,011 jobs in the bottom tercile versus 721 overall), but that contrast does not survive rescaling: expressed proportionally, the cold effect on log employment is small and imprecise both overall and within the bottom agricultural tercile (all coefficients below half a percent, *p* > 0.15). The level-scale gap reflected the mechanical fact that the least-agricultural counties are urban and larger, so a given proportional shock moves more absolute jobs there. The labor-exposure reading therefore rests on the industry-composition interaction above — proportional by construction and robust to division-by-year fixed effects — rather than on the tercile job-count difference.

## 6.4 The agricultural channel

Agriculture remains a real channel, but a narrow one. A companion difference-in-differences design that compares the 2012 drought cohort to never-exposed counties confirms the drought–income effect but attenuates the employment effect, and the drought–income effect appears event-specific — concentrated in the 2012 cohort rather than describing a general droughted-county relationship. In the recurring-treatment panel, the farm-dependence interactions are estimated imprecisely. I therefore read agriculture as one bound on the phenomenon.

## 6.5 Additional channels

Beyond morbidity and labor exposure, three further channels appear: energy burden and a supply-side provider channel, both estimated, and migration, which enters as a caveat.

**Energy burden.** Using DOE LEAD data, heat damage concentrates in high-energy-burden counties (interaction −1,380 jobs, *p* < 0.001; −$370 in income, *p* < 0.001). Energy burden — the share of household income spent on utility bills — is only weakly correlated with the CDC Social Vulnerability Index (*r* = 0.11), so it marks an independent margin rather than a relabeling of vulnerability. This is the affordability-and-adaptation mechanism of Doremus et al. (2022) and Barreca et al. (2016), in which low-income households — who bear roughly 2.6 times the energy burden (8.9 versus 3.4 percent of income) — cut necessities to pay utility bills.

**The provider-finance channel.** The demand-side mechanism has a supply-side counterpart: whether these shocks strain hospitals. On a hospital-year panel (NASHP; ∼5,100 hospitals, 2011–2023), drought is associated with lower uncompensated care and null operating margins. At first blush this is surprising, but it is consistent with both theory and the one comparable study (Audi et al., 2024–2025). The most likely explanation is that federal buffers — crop insurance and USDA disaster payments in particular, alongside Section 1135 Medicaid disaster waivers and disproportionate-share (DSH) payments — sever the chain from farm income to uninsurance to uncompensated care; the crop-insurance leg of this mechanism is directly testable with county indemnity data, which I take up below. A second, evidenced channel is that climate demand surges are revenue-positive through billable ED visits, consistent with the safety-net interaction reported next. I set aside the remaining mechanical possibilities — deferred elective care, out-migration lowering measured uncompensated care — as plausible but untested, and do not lean on them. The published rural-hospital-closure literature omits climate and agricultural variables, so this pattern fills a gap rather than contradicting an established result. This provider effect is also non-agricultural: the drought–uncompensated-care effect does not load on farm counties (interaction null) and survives essentially unchanged in the least-agricultural counties (−0.0043 versus −0.0051 overall, *p* = 0.01). Where provider strain does appear, it concentrates at safety-net hospitals. Interacting the shocks with a safety-net indicator, extreme heat raises uncompensated care more at these facilities (+0.023 at a one-year lag, *p* < 0.001; +0.020 at two years, *p* < 0.0001), consistent with providers whose operating margins are more than six times lower absorbing the demand-side morbidity surge of Section 6.2.

**Migration and selection.** Adjustment through migration bounds the scarring interpretation. Using IRS county-to-county flows, drought is associated with suggestively higher net out-migration the following year (−0.0021, *p* = 0.05), so part of the persistent drought scar may reflect who leaves rather than same-population loss. Because IRS flows undercount low-income non-filers — who are themselves among the most migration-prone households — this measured out-migration is a lower bound, so the selection share of the scar is if anything understated, which strengthens rather than weakens the caveat against reading the scar as pure same-population loss. I treat this as a caveat on the scarring interpretation rather than a distinct channel; the heat–in-migration coefficient, by contrast, is confounded by secular Sun Belt population growth and I do not read it as a shock response.

## 6.6 Summary

Taken together, these results indicate that agriculture is one channel among several. A substantial part of the climate–health-cost relationship operates outside agriculture — it is visible in a directly measured morbidity and utilization channel, in broad labor exposure, and in an energy-burden margin, none of which pass through farm income — while agriculture contributes a real but event-specific and partly selection-driven drought effect. The design bounds the agricultural channel rather than partitioning the effect into channel shares, so the claim is the weaker but well-identified one that agriculture cannot be the whole story. For risk assessment in health and insurance markets, that composition is itself the object of interest: the reduced-form effect operates well beyond the farm economy.
