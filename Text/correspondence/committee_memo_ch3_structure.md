## How the project evolved (and why)

The October 2025 proposal framed the dissertation as a demand / supply / **structural** triad:
Chapter 1 (households and insurers — premiums and medical debt), Chapter 2 (hospitals —
uncompensated care and operating performance), and Chapter 3 (a semi-structural microsimulation
calibrating premium subsidies to the evolving distribution of climate risk, with welfare and
efficiency–equity frontiers).

In response to committee feedback in April 2026 — random-effects/Hausman checks, exit dynamics,
smaller natural experiments with never-exposed controls, evidence-backed propagation channels, and
humidity — the empirical core deepened substantially. That work reorganized naturally around three
questions rather than three sectors:

- **Essay 1 — Incidence ("Deferred Costs"):** how much do climate shocks cost households, and at
  what lag? (Distributed-lag panel; the 2012-drought natural experiment with never-exposed controls
  and doubly-robust and few-treated-cluster robustness; the directly-measured Medicare
  morbidity/utilization channel.)
- **Essay 2 — Persistence ("Scarring and Compounding"):** does the damage reverse, scar, or
  compound? (Onset/persist/exit local projections; cumulative-dose response; long-run event studies.)
- **Essay 3 — Inequality ("Unequal Weather"):** who bears the cost? (Shock × social-vulnerability
  interactions replicated at county and state level; an energy-burden margin distinct from social
  vulnerability; hospital safety-net heterogeneity.)

The hospital (supply-side) results are woven through all three essays as a second ledger rather than
standing as a separate sector chapter, which I believe reads more coherently than the original
sector split.

## What this means for the proposed Chapter 3

The reorganization delivered **more** than proposed on persistence and on mechanisms, and **less** on
the structural side: the Inequality essay now occupies Chapter 3's slot, and the full structural
microsimulation (rating-area × income-bin subsidy counterfactuals, welfare accounting, California
calibration) was **not** built. I want to be transparent that this is a real departure from the
proposal rather than let it pass unremarked.

My argument for the substitution is that the reduced-form Inequality essay is empirically stronger
and more defensible than the microsimulation would have been on this data, and that the structural
model's modeling assumptions would have added risk without commensurate credibility. But this is
the committee's call, not mine to make unilaterally.

## A second, separable question (added 12 July 2026): how should Essay 3 be framed?

A July 2026 internal audit of the project's claim architecture raised a distinct structural question
I want to put to the committee alongside the one above. It does not change the three-essay count; it
changes what the third essay *is*. The two questions are separable — the committee can confirm the
three-essay structure without settling this one — but the answer here fixes Essay 3's title, its
organizing spine, and how much weight a forthcoming latent-hardship analysis must bear.

> **Given the three-essay structure, should Essay 3 be framed as (A) "Unequal Weather" — the
> distribution of climate harm across vulnerable places — or (B) "Institutional and distributional
> incidence" — who experiences the harm, and which financial systems record, absorb, or fail to
> price it?**

Both options draw on the same estimates; they differ in the object they organize them around.

**Option A — "Unequal Weather" (the current plan).** Built on shock × social-vulnerability
interactions, an energy-burden margin, a composite heat-vulnerability index, and safety-net-hospital
heterogeneity. *For:* the vulnerability amplification is robust for income, employment, and premiums
(heat turns a small employment gain into a loss; cold's income hit is several times larger in
high-vulnerability counties) and replicates across the state and county panels. *Cost:* the audit's
concern is that a hazard × exposure × vulnerability grid reads as an interaction study, leaving
Essay 3 the least independent of the three — the committee may see one paper split three ways.

**Option B — "Institutional and distributional incidence."** The same vulnerability and safety-net
results are combined with the ACA premium institutional null and the medical-debt measurement
critique into one object: when weather demonstrably raises measured morbidity and damages local
economic capacity, *which institutions actually record or price that harm, and for whom?* *For:* the
audit's central diagnosis is that this measurement-and-pricing failure — Medicare directly records
the morbidity cost, ACA premiums show no coherent local pass-through, and credit-bureau medical debt
shows its *largest* measured response where people are least vulnerable and best insured, the
opposite of where hardship should concentrate — may be the project's most original contribution,
more so than any single climate coefficient. *Cost:* it asks the premium null and the
debt-measurement results, currently framed as secondary ledgers and caveats, to carry an essay's
spine. The pre-registered observed-vs-latent-hardship analysis (does the measured debt response
shrink where coverage and credit visibility are weakest?) has now been estimated, and the honest
answer is mixed: every cell moves in the predicted direction, but under the pre-registered
significance bar only the drought × uninsurance gradient is robust — rurality and
hospital-access moderators are underpowered. Option B's spine would therefore rest on the
premium institutional null and the established uninsurance/scale-flip evidence, not on a broad
new access gradient.

**The audit recommends Option B.** I record that as a recommendation, not a decision: the choice is
the committee's and mine to make together, not the audit's.

## If the committee still wants a policy component

Rather than revive the full microsimulation — which is several months of work at high modeling risk
— I propose a **sufficient-statistics policy section**: a short, honest translation of the
already-estimated reduced-form effects into policy-relevant magnitudes, using only quantities I have
already estimated. Concretely:

1. **State the "unpriced margin" as an institutional null, not a mispricing dollar total.** The
   completed pass-through analysis finds *no coherent* ACA premium response to these shocks —
   coefficients flip sign across county, rating-area, and state levels — so there are no stable
   premium lag-responses to multiply into a mispricing figure, and I will not manufacture one.
   Instead I bound what goes unpriced against a benchmark: Medicare directly records a morbidity cost
   of roughly $112–$177 per beneficiary, while the individual market shows no local pass-through of
   it. The null is now partial rather than absolute: for drought, pass-through above 50–79% of that
   full morbidity-cost benchmark can be ruled out, while for heat and cold the response is bounded at
   roughly 5–8% of the mean premium, though not tightly.
2. **Scale the persistence results into scenario bands, not a single national total:** the
   cold-compounding job losses and — more cautiously, since credit-bureau debt is measurement-fragile
   — the drought debt scar, expressed as a small set of scenario magnitudes (a 2012-style event, a
   typical recurring shock, the direct Medicare burden), each with explicit error bands, rather than
   one headline national figure the event-specific coefficients cannot support.
3. **One targeting statement:** given the social-vulnerability and energy-burden amplification, how
   geographically concentrated the burden is (the top-decile counties' share of total harm) — the
   reduced-form answer to the proposal's subsidy-targeting question.

This honors the policy motivation of the original Chapter 3 in roughly two weeks of work rather than
several months, and it does not require any new estimator or data.

## Two smaller disclosures, for completeness

- **Headline hazards shifted** from the proposal's extreme heat / wildfire smoke / FEMA disasters to
  **cold and drought** for the natural-experiment and persistence results, with air quality in a
  minor role; heat remains central to one headline — the directly-measured Medicare
  morbidity/utilization channel — but not to the sharp causal designs. This follows the
  identification: the air-quality signal in this panel is thin (few never-exposed counties), whereas
  the cold and drought results survive the full robustness battery. I plan to acknowledge this
  demotion explicitly in the text.
- **The premium → medical-debt mediation** promised in Chapter 1 (the "first joint elasticities")
  has now been estimated — and it changed the story rather than confirming it. There is no coherent
  ACA premium response for debt to be mediated through (premium coefficients flip sign across
  geographic levels), and a decomposition shows roughly 92–99% of each shock → debt effect survives
  premium adjustment. The result is therefore an institutional null — regulated pricing does not
  locally price the shock — not the positive joint elasticity the proposal anticipated. I flag the
  reversal rather than let the earlier framing stand.
