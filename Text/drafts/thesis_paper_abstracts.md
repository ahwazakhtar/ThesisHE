# Climate Shocks and the Financial Health of American Households
### A Three-Essay Dissertation: Incidence, Persistence, and Inequality

---

## Dissertation Abstract (Umbrella)

Climate shocks impose costs on households long after the weather normalizes, yet these costs are seldom measured financially, traced over time, or mapped onto those least able to absorb them. This dissertation develops the financial epidemiology of climate exposure in U.S. health markets through three linked essays, built on a harmonized state and county panel (≈1996–2025) that integrates NOAA climate data, PRISM humidity, EPA air quality, and CDC vulnerability indices with insurance premiums, medical debt, hospital bad debt, and local economic outcomes. Essay 1 establishes *incidence*: using distributed-lag fixed-effects models, a never-exposed-control difference-in-differences design, and local projections, it locates the financial cost of climate stress in lagged household outcomes rather than contemporaneous system spending. Treated as a sharp natural experiment, the 2012 drought lowered per-capita income by about $1,311 in first-onset counties—with a fragile, event-specific employment decline alongside it—and county Medicare records (65+ and disabled, 2014–2023) provide separate, direct evidence of a non-agricultural morbidity and utilization channel; the same burden operates substantially outside agriculture, carried by heat's interaction with local labor and energy-burden exposure. The secondary financial ledgers record this harm unevenly: medical debt responds at a one- to two-year lag but is measurement-fragile, and ACA premiums show no coherent local pass-through. Essay 2 turns to *persistence*, decomposing shocks into onset, persistence, and exit transitions and estimating cumulative-dose responses: drought debt scars (partly through selective out-migration), cold employment losses compound with repeated exposure, and heat saturates—so the policy-relevant horizon is hazard-specific. Essay 3 addresses *inequality*, interacting shocks with the CDC Social Vulnerability Index: climate harm to income, employment, and premiums is amplified in structurally vulnerable communities; a distinct energy-burden margin, only weakly correlated with social vulnerability, concentrates heat's employment damage in high-burden counties; and on the supply side the strain lands on safety-net hospitals, while credit-bureau medical debt is measurement-fragile. Together the essays argue that environmental risk assessment in health and insurance markets must price not only the lagged incidence of climate stress, but its persistence and its unequal distribution—triangulating across financial ledgers that differ in whether they even record or price the harm, not tracing a single fully identified propagation chain.

## Essay 1 — Deferred Costs: The Lagged Financial Burden of Climate Shocks in U.S. Health Markets

Climate shocks strain household finances slowly, surfacing in lagged household outcomes rather than in contemporaneous system spending. Using a harmonized U.S. state and county panel from 1996 to 2025 that links NOAA climate and EPA air-quality data to insurance premiums, medical debt, and health spending, I estimate distributed-lag fixed-effects models and corroborate them with a difference-in-differences design that uses never-exposed counties as controls and with dynamic local projections. I treat the 2012 drought as a sharp natural experiment: in the first-onset counties (Georgia, the Mountain West, and the Plains) relative to never-exposed controls, per-capita income fell by about $1,311—robust to wild-cluster and randomization inference and to a flat two-decade pre-trend, and strengthening to about −$1,451 under a doubly-robust estimator. This is the effect of the 2012 event rather than a general drought-response function: under the doubly-robust multi-cohort estimator the pooled average is null both at onset and over the long run. An accompanying employment decline of roughly 2,000 jobs is fragile—it attenuates by about 58 percent under the doubly-robust estimator and reverses sign in the pooled estimator—so I report it as event-specific, not as a co-headline. Systemic per-capita health spending follows income and unemployment rather than the weather. For separate, direct evidence of a morbidity channel — parallel to, not mediating, the income result — I add county Medicare records (CMS Geographic Variation, 65+ and disabled, 2014–2023): extreme heat raises standardized Medicare spending by $112 per beneficiary contemporaneously and $177 a year later and raises emergency-department visits by roughly 8 to 10 per 1,000, reproducing Deryugina et al. within panel—a non-agricultural channel that needs no farm-income intermediary and that I do not extend to working-age household debt. The lagged burden operates substantially outside agriculture: heat's interaction with a county's climate-exposed non-farm labor share and with its energy-burden gradient predicts (log) employment losses that survive division-by-year effects, Conley standard errors, and a specification horse-race. The secondary financial ledgers record the harm unevenly. Cold shocks raise the medical-debt share by about 1.35 percentage points one year later (state fixed effects, p=0.012), and extreme drought raises it by roughly 0.5 points after two years at the county level (significant only there, not in the state panel), but medical debt is measurement-fragile. ACA benchmark premiums show no coherent local pass-through: coefficients flip sign across county, rating-area, and state levels, within-state responses stay below roughly 7 to 8 percent of the mean premium, and 92 to 99 percent of each shock's debt response survives premium adjustment—consistent with a single statewide risk pool, unit-cost-only geographic rating, and federal risk adjustment rather than local repricing. These results locate the financial cost of climate stress in lagged household outcomes and identify the environmental lag structure as a margin that regulated insurance pricing does not coherently price locally — a bound that is tight for drought and looser for heat and cold.

## Essay 2 — Scarring and Compounding: The Persistence of Climate-Driven Health Costs

Whether a changing climate raises health costs over the long run depends on what happens once a shock ends—whether the damage reverses, leaves a scar, or compounds as shocks recur. Using a U.S. county panel from 2011 to 2023, I decompose each climate shock into its onset, its persistence, and its exit, test whether entering a shock and leaving it offset one another, and estimate how outcomes respond to the cumulative number of shock-years a county has accumulated. The dynamics are shock-specific. Drought debt scars: the debt accrued during a drought does not unwind once the drought ends—a two-year onset–exit asymmetry of about +0.019 (p<0.001) that survives premium adjustment and multiple-testing correction. Part of that persistent gap is composition rather than same-population loss—IRS county-to-county flows show drought raises net out-migration the following year (suggestive, p≈0.05)—so the scar is a lower bound, and because it is measured in credit-bureau debt it is measurement-fragile. Cold damage compounds: in an unweighted binned contrast the tenth cumulative cold-year reduces county employment by about 5,500 more than the first, and a staggered (Callaway–Sant'Anna) difference-in-differences shows an employment gap widening to roughly 5,000 jobs and a medical-debt-share gap of about 4.9 points a decade after onset; the smooth quadratic dose term is flat, so the compounding rests on the binned and event-study estimators. Heat damage instead saturates into a fixed level difference rather than growing, and chronic extreme drought is so rare that drought is best understood as episodic. The cold-compounding result survives adjustment for humidity and population ageing and holds under a stricter definition of extreme temperature. These results show that the horizon over which climate raises health costs is itself hazard-specific, so contemporaneous exposure measures understate the burden of recurring cold.

## Essay 3 — Unequal Weather: Social Vulnerability and the Distribution of Climate Health Costs

The same climate shock need not impose the same cost everywhere; where households are already economically and socially fragile, a shock can tip them from coping to crisis. Building a hazard × exposure × vulnerability index for a U.S. county panel from 2011 to 2023, I interact climate shocks with the CDC Social Vulnerability Index, compare the marginal effect of a shock in the least and most vulnerable counties, and mirror every estimate at the state level. Structural vulnerability amplifies the real-economy harm of climate. In the most vulnerable counties extreme heat costs jobs, turning a small employment gain into a loss (interaction p=0.001); cold's hit to per-capita income is roughly eight times larger than in the least vulnerable counties, though this interaction is only marginal (p=0.056); and drought, at a two-year lag, pushes benchmark insurance premiums up in vulnerable counties rather than down. A composite heat-vulnerability index lowers median household income by about $435 per standard deviation (p=0.0002)—a composite-index descriptive rather than a causal per-standard-deviation price, and one that flips sign against per-capita income—and the country absorbs on the order of seventy to one hundred and five million person-years of extreme-heat exposure each year, several times the cold burden. Beyond the vulnerability index, a distinct affordability margin operates through energy burden: using DOE LEAD data, heat's employment damage concentrates in high-energy-burden counties and survives a joint horse-race and division-by-year effects, and because county energy burden is only weakly correlated with the Social Vulnerability Index (r = 0.11) it marks an independent dimension of exposure rather than a relabeling of vulnerability; its income margin, by contrast, is only suggestive. The same unequal incidence appears on the supply side, where extreme heat raises uncompensated care most at safety-net hospitals—measured at the hospital's location county rather than its patient catchment, and robust to winsorizing the underlying hospital accounts; the cold interaction runs the other way. Credit-bureau medical debt is the exception: it concentrates in less-vulnerable and less-uninsured areas and flips sign across geographic scales, because bureau debt requires insurance, a billed encounter, and a credit file—a measurement artifact of how debt is recorded rather than evidence against unequal harm, and one I therefore exclude from the amplification result. These findings identify social vulnerability as a first-order margin in the real-economy health cost of climate, and caution that credit-bureau debt is least informative about hardship precisely where insurance coverage and credit visibility are weakest.

**JEL:** I14, Q56, Q54, I18
