# Climate Shocks and the Financial Health of American Households
### A Three-Essay Dissertation: Incidence, Persistence, and Inequality

---

## Dissertation Abstract (Umbrella)

Climate shocks impose costs on households long after the weather normalizes, yet these costs are seldom measured financially, traced over time, or mapped onto those least able to absorb them. This dissertation develops the financial epidemiology of climate exposure in U.S. health markets through three linked essays, built on a harmonized state and county panel (≈1996–2025) that integrates NOAA climate data, PRISM humidity, EPA air quality, and CDC vulnerability indices with insurance premiums, medical debt, hospital bad debt, and local economic outcomes. Essay 1 establishes *incidence*: using distributed-lag fixed-effects models, a never-exposed-control difference-in-differences design, and local projections, it shows cold and drought raise medical debt and premiums at a one- to two-year lag, operating through a household income channel rather than systemic spending. Essay 2 turns to *persistence*, decomposing shocks into onset, persistence, and exit transitions and estimating cumulative-dose responses: drought debt scars, cold employment losses compound with repeated exposure, and heat saturates—so the policy-relevant horizon is hazard-specific. Essay 3 addresses *inequality*, constructing a hazard × exposure × vulnerability index that interacts shocks with the CDC Social Vulnerability Index; climate harm to income, employment, and premiums is amplified in structurally vulnerable communities, while credit-bureau medical debt is shown to be measurement-fragile. Together the essays argue that environmental risk assessment in health and insurance markets must price not only the lagged incidence of climate stress, but its persistence and its unequal distribution.

## Essay 1 — Deferred Costs: The Lagged Financial Burden of Climate Shocks in U.S. Health Markets

**Objective.** This essay quantifies the delayed financial impact of climate shocks—drought and temperature anomalies—on U.S. health-insurance premiums, medical-debt incidence, and systemic health expenditure.

**Data and methods.** I assemble a harmonized state and county panel (≈1996–2025) linking NOAA climate and EPA air-quality data to financial outcomes from MEPS-IC, HIX Compare, the Urban Institute, and CMS. Exposure is binned into shocks using state-specific temperature Z-scores and PDSI drought thresholds. The core design is a distributed-lag two-way fixed-effects model (unit and year effects, state-clustered errors) at zero-, one-, and two-year lags, corroborated by a natural-experiment difference-in-differences using never-exposed counties as controls, dynamic local projections, and random-effects/Hausman checks.

**Results.** Climate stress transmits to financial fragility with a characteristic delay. Cold shocks raise the medical-debt share by ≈1.1 percentage points after one year (p<0.002); extreme drought raises it ≈0.7 points after two years, alongside an ≈$18.50 rise in real employee premiums consistent with the annual actuarial cycle. Exploiting the 2012 Midwest drought as a sharp natural experiment, treated counties show a $1,311 per-capita-income decline (p=0.027) and a 2,053-job employment decline (p<0.001) versus never-exposed controls, identifying the income channel that feeds medical debt. Systemic per-capita expenditure tracks income and unemployment, not climate.

**Conclusion.** Climate-driven health-financial costs are lagged, not contemporaneous, and concentrated in household outcomes, implying that insurers and policymakers should price environmental lag structures into risk assessment.

**Keywords:** climate shocks, medical debt, insurance premiums, distributed-lag fixed effects, difference-in-differences.

## Essay 2 — Scarring and Compounding: The Persistence of Climate-Driven Health Costs

**Objective.** Given that climate shocks raise household health-financial burden, this essay asks how that burden evolves: does it reverse when the shock ends, leave a permanent scar, or compound with repeated exposure?

**Data and methods.** Using a U.S. county panel (2011–2023) of climate shocks linked to medical debt, hospital bad debt, income, and employment, I decompose each shock into onset, persistence, and exit transitions and test the symmetry hypothesis that entering and leaving a shock offset (β_onset + β_exit = 0); estimate cumulative-shock-year dose-response models in linear, quadratic, and binned form; characterize continuously-exposed sub-populations; and cross-validate with a Callaway–Sant'Anna staggered difference-in-differences against never-exposed controls. Robustness is assessed against humidity (PRISM dew point), demographic change (ACS migration, ageing, tenure), and the extreme-temperature threshold.

**Results.** Persistence is shock-specific. Drought debt scars—debt accrued during drought is not undone on exit (two-year asymmetry +0.018, p=0.0015). Cold damage compounds—the tenth cumulative cold-year reduces county employment by ≈5,700 more than the first (p<0.001), echoed by a Callaway–Sant'Anna employment gap of ≈−4,980 and a +4.9-point debt-share gap by event-time ten. Heat saturates into a standing level difference, and chronic extreme drought is effectively absent, making drought episodic. The findings survive humidity adjustment, are unmediated by demographic change, and persist under a stricter threshold.

**Conclusion.** The policy-relevant horizon differs by hazard—transient-but-scarring for drought, cumulatively compounding for cold—so contemporaneous exposure measures understate the long-run burden.

**Keywords:** persistence, hysteresis, local projections, cumulative dose-response, Callaway–Sant'Anna.

## Essay 3 — Unequal Weather: Social Vulnerability and the Distribution of Climate Health Costs

**Objective.** This essay asks who bears the financial cost of climate shocks, testing whether structural vulnerability amplifies the climate-to-health-cost relationship.

**Data and methods.** Adapting the climate-health indicator tradition, I build a hazard × exposure × vulnerability index combining the climate hazard, population, and the CDC/ATSDR Social Vulnerability Index for a U.S. county panel (2011–2023). The primary specification interacts climate shocks with vulnerability (Shock × SVI) in a fixed-effects model, recovering the marginal shock effect at low versus high vulnerability; secondary analyses use a composite scalar index and report Lancet-style population-weighted person-years of extreme-temperature exposure. Every result is mirrored at the state level for cross-scale consistency.

**Results.** Structural vulnerability amplifies the real-economy harm of climate shocks. In the most vulnerable counties, extreme heat costs jobs (the employment effect flips from +878 to −184, p=0.001), cold's per-capita-income loss is roughly eight times larger (−$56 to −$472), and drought pushes benchmark ACA premiums upward; a composite heat-vulnerability index lowers median household income by $435 per standard deviation (p=0.0002). The United States bears ≈70–105 million heat person-years of exposure annually, 3–5× the cold burden. Credit-bureau medical debt is the exception—concentrating in less-vulnerable areas and reversing sign across aggregation levels—which I show is a measurement artifact rather than evidence against unequal harm.

**Conclusion.** Climate health-financial risk falls disproportionately on already-disadvantaged communities; risk assessment should be vulnerability-weighted, and debt-based welfare metrics interpreted with care.

**Keywords:** environmental justice, social vulnerability index, exposure index, effect modification, Lancet Countdown.
