# Climate Shocks and the Financial Health of American Households
### A Three-Essay Dissertation: Incidence, Persistence, and Inequality

---

## Dissertation Abstract (Umbrella)

Climate shocks impose costs on households long after the weather normalizes, yet these costs are seldom measured financially, traced over time, or mapped onto those least able to absorb them. This dissertation develops the financial epidemiology of climate exposure in U.S. health markets through three linked essays, built on a harmonized state and county panel (≈1996–2025) that integrates NOAA climate data, PRISM humidity, EPA air quality, and CDC vulnerability indices with insurance premiums, medical debt, hospital bad debt, and local economic outcomes. Essay 1 establishes *incidence*: using distributed-lag fixed-effects models, a never-exposed-control difference-in-differences design, and local projections, it locates the financial cost of climate stress in lagged household outcomes rather than contemporaneous system spending—the 2012 drought lowered per-capita income by about $1,311 in first-onset counties (with a fragile, event-specific employment decline alongside it), county Medicare records place a directly-measured non-agricultural morbidity channel beneath it, and the secondary ledgers record the harm unevenly (medical debt is measurement-fragile; ACA premiums show no coherent local pass-through). Essay 2 turns to *persistence*, decomposing shocks into onset, persistence, and exit transitions and estimating cumulative-dose responses: drought debt scars (partly through selective out-migration), cold employment losses compound with repeated exposure, and heat saturates—so the policy-relevant horizon is hazard-specific. Essay 3 addresses *inequality*, interacting shocks with the CDC Social Vulnerability Index: climate harm to income, employment, and premiums is amplified in structurally vulnerable communities, while credit-bureau medical debt is measurement-fragile. Together the essays argue that environmental risk assessment in health and insurance markets must price not only the lagged incidence of climate stress, but its persistence and its unequal distribution—triangulating across financial ledgers that differ in whether they even record or price the harm, not tracing a single fully identified propagation chain.

## Essay 1 — Deferred Costs: The Lagged Financial Burden of Climate Shocks in U.S. Health Markets

**Objective.** This essay quantifies the delayed financial impact of climate shocks—drought and temperature anomalies—on U.S. households, locating the incidence first in local income and directly-measured morbidity and then in the secondary financial ledgers of medical debt and health-insurance premiums.

**Data and methods.** I assemble a harmonized state and county panel (≈1996–2025) linking NOAA climate and EPA air-quality data to financial outcomes from MEPS-IC, HIX Compare, the Urban Institute, and CMS, and add county Medicare records (CMS Geographic Variation, 2014–2023) to measure morbidity directly. Exposure is binned into shocks using state-specific temperature Z-scores and PDSI drought thresholds. The core design is a distributed-lag two-way fixed-effects model (unit and year effects, state-clustered errors) at zero-, one-, and two-year lags, corroborated by a natural-experiment difference-in-differences using never-exposed counties as controls, dynamic local projections, and a doubly-robust (DRDID) estimator.

**Results.** The financial cost of climate stress surfaces in lagged household outcomes rather than contemporaneous system spending. Exploiting the 2012 drought as a sharp natural experiment, first-onset counties (Georgia, the Mountain West, and the Plains) show a per-capita-income decline of about $1,311 (p=0.027) versus never-exposed controls—robust to wild-cluster and randomization inference and a flat two-decade pre-trend, and strengthening to about −$1,451 under DRDID; this is the effect of the 2012 event, as the pooled multi-cohort average is null under the doubly-robust estimator at onset and over the long run. The accompanying employment decline of roughly 2,000 jobs is fragile—it attenuates by about 58 percent under DRDID and reverses sign in the pooled estimator—and is reported as event-specific, not a co-headline. Systemic per-capita expenditure tracks income and unemployment, not climate. County Medicare records place the income channel in directly-measured, non-agricultural morbidity: extreme heat raises standardized Medicare spending $112 per beneficiary contemporaneously and $177 a year later and raises emergency-department visits by roughly 8–10 per 1,000 (65+/disabled population). In the secondary ledgers, cold shocks raise the medical-debt share by about 1.35 pp after one year (state FE, p=0.012) and drought raises it by roughly 0.5 pp after two years at the county level only, but debt is measurement-fragile; ACA benchmark premiums show no coherent local pass-through, with coefficients that flip sign across county, rating-area, and state and within-state responses below roughly 7–8 percent of the mean premium.

**Conclusion.** Climate-driven financial costs are lagged and concentrated in household outcomes; the environmental lag structure is a margin that regulated insurance pricing does not locally price, so risk assessment must reckon with an incidence that its priced instruments largely fail to record.

**Keywords:** climate shocks, medical debt, insurance premiums, Medicare morbidity, distributed-lag fixed effects, difference-in-differences.

## Essay 2 — Scarring and Compounding: The Persistence of Climate-Driven Health Costs

**Objective.** Given that climate shocks raise household health-financial burden, this essay asks how that burden evolves: does it reverse when the shock ends, leave a scar, or compound with repeated exposure?

**Data and methods.** Using a U.S. county panel (2011–2023) of climate shocks linked to medical debt, hospital bad debt, income, and employment, I decompose each shock into onset, persistence, and exit transitions and test the symmetry hypothesis that entering and leaving a shock offset (β_onset + β_exit = 0); estimate cumulative-shock-year dose-response models in linear, quadratic, and binned form; characterize continuously-exposed sub-populations; and cross-validate with a Callaway–Sant'Anna staggered difference-in-differences against never-exposed controls. Robustness is assessed against humidity (PRISM dew point), demographic change (ACS migration, ageing, tenure), and the extreme-temperature threshold.

**Results.** Persistence is shock-specific. Drought debt scars—debt accrued during drought is not undone on exit (two-year onset–exit asymmetry +0.019, p<0.001)—but part of that gap is composition, since drought raises net out-migration the following year (suggestive, p≈0.05), so the scar is a lower bound and, being measured in credit-bureau debt, is measurement-fragile. Cold damage compounds—in an unweighted binned contrast the tenth cumulative cold-year reduces county employment by about 5,500 more than the first, echoed by a Callaway–Sant'Anna employment gap of roughly −5,000 and a +4.9-point debt-share gap by event-time ten; the smooth quadratic dose term is flat, so the compounding rests on the binned and event-study estimators. Heat saturates into a standing level difference, and chronic extreme drought is effectively absent, making drought episodic. The cold-compounding result survives humidity adjustment, is unmediated by demographic change, and persists under a stricter threshold.

**Conclusion.** The policy-relevant horizon differs by hazard—transient-but-scarring for drought, cumulatively compounding for cold—so contemporaneous exposure measures understate the long-run burden of recurring cold.

**Keywords:** persistence, hysteresis, local projections, cumulative dose-response, Callaway–Sant'Anna.

## Essay 3 — Unequal Weather: Social Vulnerability and the Distribution of Climate Health Costs

**Objective.** This essay asks who bears the financial cost of climate shocks, testing whether structural vulnerability amplifies the climate-to-health-cost relationship.

**Data and methods.** Adapting the climate-health indicator tradition, I build a hazard × exposure × vulnerability index combining the climate hazard, population, and the CDC/ATSDR Social Vulnerability Index for a U.S. county panel (2011–2023). The primary specification interacts climate shocks with vulnerability (Shock × SVI) in a fixed-effects model, recovering the marginal shock effect at low versus high vulnerability; secondary analyses use a composite scalar index, an energy-burden margin (DOE LEAD), a hospital supply-side interaction, and Lancet-style population-weighted person-years of extreme-temperature exposure. Every real-economy result is mirrored at the state level for cross-scale consistency.

**Results.** Structural vulnerability amplifies the real-economy harm of climate shocks. In the most vulnerable counties, extreme heat costs jobs (the employment effect flips from +878 to −184, interaction p=0.001), cold's per-capita-income loss is roughly eight times larger (−$56 to −$472, though this interaction is marginal at p=0.056), and drought at a two-year lag pushes benchmark ACA premiums upward; a composite heat-vulnerability index lowers median household income by about $435 per standard deviation (p=0.0002, a composite-index descriptive that flips sign against per-capita income). A distinct energy-burden margin concentrates heat's employment damage in high-energy-burden counties and is only weakly correlated with the SVI (r=0.11), and on the supply side extreme heat raises uncompensated care most at safety-net hospitals (measured at the provider's location county). The United States bears ≈70–105 million heat person-years of exposure annually, several times the cold burden. Credit-bureau medical debt is the exception—concentrating in less-vulnerable, less-uninsured areas and reversing sign across aggregation levels—which I treat as a measurement artifact and exclude from the amplification result rather than evidence against unequal harm.

**Conclusion.** The real-economy health-financial cost of climate falls disproportionately on already-disadvantaged communities, so risk assessment should be vulnerability-weighted; credit-bureau debt, by contrast, is least informative about hardship precisely where access and visibility are weakest.

**Keywords:** environmental justice, social vulnerability index, exposure index, energy burden, effect modification, Lancet Countdown.
