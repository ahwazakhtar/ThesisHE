# Dissertation Writing and Framing Plan

## Climate Shocks and the Financial Health of American Households

**Date:** July 12, 2026  
**Assumption:** The committee has approved the three-essay dissertation structure and no longer requires the proposed structural Chapter 3 as a condition of completion.  
**Purpose:** Convert the completed empirical work into a strong, coherent applied-economics dissertation. This plan prioritizes writing, contribution discipline, essay differentiation, and defensible interpretation over further specification search.

---

## 1. Target dissertation identity

### Recommended umbrella framing

The dissertation should study the **economic incidence, persistence, and institutional visibility of climate-related health and financial stress**.

The central thesis is:

> Climate shocks impose economically meaningful costs through local income, labor-market, and healthcare-utilization margins. These costs can persist or compound, concentrate in vulnerable places, and remain imperfectly recorded or priced by medical-debt, insurance-premium, and hospital-accounting systems.

This framing is preferable to a claim that the dissertation identifies one complete chain from weather to morbidity to debt to premiums. The data instead provide disciplined triangulation across several economic and health-sector ledgers.

### What the dissertation should claim to contribute

The dissertation makes four connected contributions:

1. **Causal incidence:** A well-defended natural experiment shows that the 2012 drought caused a substantial decline in county per-capita income.
2. **Dynamic incidence:** Climate costs differ in whether they reverse, scar, saturate, or compound as exposure recurs.
3. **Distributional incidence:** Real-economy and provider burdens are amplified by social vulnerability, labor exposure, energy burden, and safety-net status.
4. **Institutional visibility:** Direct utilization responds more clearly than several financial ledgers; credit-bureau debt is selective, and ACA premiums exhibit no coherent local pass-through.

### What the dissertation should not claim

Do not claim that:

- The 2012 estimate is a universal drought response function.
- Medicare morbidity mediates the 2012 drought-income effect.
- Medical debt is a comprehensive welfare measure.
- ACA premiums are generally unaffected by climate costs in an equivalence-test sense; the tight bound is drought-specific.
- Cold employment compounding is estimator-invariant.
- The essays identify a complete structural propagation chain.
- The sufficient-statistics exercise is a national causal welfare calculation.

### Chapter 1 — General introduction

The introduction should make the dissertation one intellectual project without collapsing the essays into one paper.

Recommended sections:

1. Motivation: climate damages extend beyond contemporaneous mortality and output.
2. Economic problem: incidence is delayed, persistent, distributed unevenly, and imperfectly recorded.
3. Conceptual framework: shocks, household capacity, healthcare use, financial ledgers, and institutions.
4. Data contribution.
5. Research designs and estimand ladder.
6. Four principal findings.
7. Contributions to environmental, health, labor, and household-finance economics.
8. Dissertation roadmap.

### Chapter 2 — Essay 1: Incidence and institutional recording

Working title:

> **Deferred Costs: Climate Shocks, Local Economic Capacity, and Health-Market Ledgers**

Primary question:

> What economic and healthcare-utilization costs follow climate shocks, and which financial institutions record or price them?

Primary empirical centerpiece:

- 2012 drought → county per-capita income.

Secondary evidence:

- Medicare spending and emergency utilization as direct morbidity evidence.
- Medical debt as a delayed but selective financial ledger.
- ACA premiums as an institutional pass-through test.
- Employment as a fragile, event-specific secondary outcome.

### Chapter 3 — Essay 2: Persistence

Working title:

> **Scarring and Compounding: The Dynamic Economic Costs of Recurring Climate Exposure**

Primary question:

> When a climate shock ends or recurs, do its economic effects reverse, leave a scar, saturate, or compound?

Primary empirical contribution:

- Transition decomposition: onset, persistence, and exit.
- Symmetry tests as the operational definition of reversibility.
- Cumulative exposure and recurring-treatment evidence.

Headline patterns:

- Drought-related measured debt exhibits exit asymmetry, subject to measurement and migration caveats.
- Cold-related employment effects compound in binned and long-run event-study estimates, but not in the smooth quadratic dose term.
- Heat effects appear to saturate rather than compound.

### Chapter 4 — Essay 3: Distributional and institutional incidence

Recommended title:

> **Unequal and Unrecorded: The Distribution of Climate-Related Economic and Health-System Costs**

Primary question:

> Who bears climate-related economic costs, and when do the institutions used to measure financial hardship fail to record them?

This combines the strongest parts of the inequality and institutional-incidence framings without pretending they are separate papers.

Primary evidence:

- Shock × SVI heterogeneity for real-economy outcomes.
- Energy burden and non-farm labor exposure as distinct moderators.
- Heat × safety-net hospital uncompensated care.
- Drought × uninsurance as the narrow, multiplicity-robust credit-visibility gradient.
- Burden concentration by vulnerability and exposure.

### Chapter 5 — Sufficient-statistics policy synthesis

This should be a bounded synthesis chapter or substantial concluding section, not a structural model.

It should report:

- A 2012-style drought event burden scenario.
- A direct Medicare morbidity-cost scenario.
- A recurring-exposure scenario where supported.
- The share of measured burden concentrated in high-vulnerability counties.
- Comparison with agricultural disaster-transfer flows where units permit.
- ACA pass-through bounds, separated by hazard.

Every result should be labeled as an event calculation, recurring-panel scenario, accounting exercise, or causal estimate.

### Chapter 6 — General conclusion

The conclusion should synthesize rather than repeat coefficient lists.

Recommended structure:

1. What is now known.
2. What conventional ledgers miss.
3. Implications for climate adaptation and health-finance policy.
4. External-validity and measurement limits.
5. Research agenda.

## 3. Essay differentiation strategy

Each essay must have a different economic object, not merely a different subset of coefficients.

| Essay | Economic object | Primary estimand | Lead design | Lead outcome | Distinct contribution |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Incidence | Immediate and lagged costs | Event-specific ITT and direct utilization responses | 2012 2×2 DiD; administrative Medicare panel | Per-capita income; Medicare utilization | Establishes that climate stress affects economic capacity and directly measured healthcare use |
| 2. Persistence | Adjustment over time | Onset–exit asymmetry and cumulative exposure response | Transition FE, LP, cumulative dose, recurring-treatment comparison | Employment; measured debt | Distinguishes reversal, scarring, saturation, and compounding |
| 3. Distribution | Allocation and observability | Heterogeneous marginal effects and burden concentration | Shock × baseline vulnerability; provider heterogeneity | Income/employment; hospital uncompensated care | Shows who bears costs and when standard financial measures under-record them |

### Cross-essay reuse rules

- The same result should have only one substantive home.
- Other essays may cite it in one or two sentences but should not reproduce its full table.
- Essay 1 owns the 2012 drought design and Medicare incidence.
- Essay 2 owns transition symmetry, dose, and long-run recurring-exposure results.
- Essay 3 owns SVI, energy burden, labor exposure, safety-net hospitals, and visibility gradients.
- The general introduction owns the integrated ledger framework.
- The policy synthesis owns aggregation and concentration accounting.

## 4. Claim hierarchy and prose rules

`Plans/master_evidence_table.md` is binding. Each manuscript claim should carry one of four implicit statuses.

### Headline

May appear in the abstract, introduction, conclusion, and main tables.

Recommended headline set:

1. 2012 drought → per-capita income loss.
2. Direct Medicare morbidity/utilization responses to temperature exposure.
3. Cold employment compounding, explicitly qualified by estimator dependence.
4. No coherent ACA local pass-through, with hazard-specific bounds.
5. Vulnerability amplification for selected real-economy outcomes.

### Confirmatory

Supports a headline but does not become a separate contribution:

- Wild-cluster and randomization inference.
- Long BEA pretrend.
- Leave-one-treated-state-out analysis.
- Placebo onset distribution.
- Humidity and threshold sensitivity.
- Conley and harder-FE variants.

### Mechanism-supporting

Use “consistent with,” “loads on,” “supports,” or “provides direct evidence of a separate channel.” Avoid “operates through” unless mediation is identified.

- Medicare morbidity.
- Non-farm labor exposure.
- Energy burden.
- Safety-net hospital interaction.
- RMA indemnity first stage.

### Exploratory or measurement critique

Keep out of headline paragraphs unless the critique itself is the contribution:

- Credit-bureau medical-debt heterogeneity.
- Composite CHEI coefficient.
- Reversed Medicaid-expansion interactions.
- Hospital drought accounting responses.
- Null or unstable premium coefficients at individual geographic levels.

### Required language discipline

Use:

- “The 2012 drought event reduced…”
- “The immediate pooled response is consistent across cohorts, but the long-run average is not.”
- “Provides separate direct evidence of morbidity and utilization effects.”
- “No coherent pass-through across institutional levels.”
- “The data rule out morbidity-scale pass-through for drought; heat and cold remain less tightly bounded.”
- “Measured credit-bureau debt.”
- “Exposure at the hospital’s location county.”
- “Consistent with persistence, migration, or changing composition.”

Avoid:

- “Drought generally causes…”
- “Medicare explains the income effect.”
- “Medical debt measures welfare loss.”
- “Insurers leave climate costs unpriced” without the hazard and market qualification.
- “Cold employment losses definitively compound.”
- “The mechanism is…” when the evidence is interaction-based.

## 5. General introduction blueprint

Target length: approximately 25–35 double-spaced pages, excluding references.

### Opening sequence

The first five pages should establish:

1. Climate shocks affect health and economic production.
2. Their financial incidence can emerge after the exposure year.
3. Persistence determines whether a temporary shock creates a temporary or durable loss.
4. Existing administrative measures may not record the same population or cost.
5. The dissertation studies incidence, dynamics, distribution, and institutional visibility in one harmonized framework.

### Conceptual framework

Use a simple framework rather than a structural model:

```text
Climate exposure
  ├─→ health and utilization
  ├─→ labor productivity and local income
  ├─→ household liquidity and financial distress
  └─→ provider demand and accounting outcomes

Observed through different institutions:
  Medicare | employers | ACA rating areas | credit bureaus | hospitals
```

Explain that each ledger has different eligibility, reporting, geography, and timing. This motivates why inconsistent responses can be economically informative rather than merely inconvenient.

### Contribution positioning

Position the dissertation at the intersection of:

- Climate and environmental economics
- Health economics
- Labor and local public economics
- Household finance
- Economics of administrative measurement

The literature section should organize around questions, not datasets:

1. Short-run health and productivity effects of temperature and pollution.
2. Persistent local effects of environmental and economic shocks.
3. Insurance pricing and regulated risk pooling.
4. Distributional exposure and environmental justice.
5. Measurement of hardship through administrative systems.

## 7. Essay 2 writing plan

Target length: approximately 40–55 pages plus appendix.

### Central framing

The essay is about **adjustment regimes**, not merely lagged coefficients.

Define four economically distinct patterns:

- **Reversal:** exit offsets onset.
- **Scarring:** exit fails to offset onset.
- **Saturation:** effects persist at a level but do not grow with dose.
- **Compounding:** marginal harm grows with repeated exposure.

### Main sections

1. Why persistence matters for welfare and adaptation
2. Transition framework and estimands
3. Data and recurring exposure
4. Onset–exit symmetry results
5. Cumulative-dose results
6. Long-run cohort and recurring-treatment comparisons
7. Migration, composition, and survivorship
8. Interpretation by hazard
9. Conclusion

### Main tables

1. Transition frequencies and support
2. Onset, persistence, and exit coefficients
3. Symmetry tests
4. Cumulative-dose contrasts
5. Cross-estimator comparison

### Main figures

1. Conceptual diagrams of reversal/scarring/saturation/compounding
2. Drought transition response
3. Cold cumulative-dose response with both binned and smooth specifications
4. Heat saturation profile
5. Cross-estimator comparison showing agreement and disagreement

### Required honesty box

Include a short subsection titled “What identifies compounding?” It should state explicitly:

- The binned contrast and long-run event-study estimates support compounding.
- The smooth quadratic dose term is flat.
- Levels-based employment outcomes are sensitive to county size.
- Migration and sample composition can contribute to long-run gaps.
- The result is a pattern supported by particular estimators, not an estimator-invariant law.

## 9. Policy synthesis plan

Target length: approximately 20–30 pages if a chapter; 10–15 pages if integrated into the general conclusion.

### Questions

1. How large are the costs under transparent event and exposure scenarios?
2. Where are those costs concentrated?
3. Which institutions price or absorb them?
4. What can policy target without requiring a structural welfare model?

### Required scenario separation

| Scenario | Coefficient source | Permitted interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| 2012-style drought event | 2012 DiD income ATT | Cost of an event with the observed design and treated geography |
| Immediate multi-cohort drought | pooled event-time-zero estimate | Short-run response across observed cohorts |
| Medicare morbidity | direct spending/utilization coefficients | Administrative healthcare burden for Medicare population |
| Recurring cold exposure | dose/event-study estimates | Scenario range, explicitly estimator-dependent |
| Distributional targeting | exposure × population × vulnerability accounting | Descriptive concentration, not causal welfare weights |

### Uncertainty

- Propagate coefficient uncertainty.
- Keep coefficient and exposure uncertainty separate where possible.
- Report sensitivity bands rather than a preferred single national total.
- Never average incompatible estimands.
- Distinguish dollars, jobs, utilization, and debt shares rather than forcing them into one welfare metric.

## 10. Table and figure strategy

### Main-text rule

Each essay should contain no more than:

- 6–8 main tables
- 4–6 main figures
- 2–3 headline findings

Everything else belongs in an appendix.

### One-result-one-home rule

Create a dissertation exhibit registry with:

- Exhibit number
- Essay owner
- Generating script
- Output file
- Evidence-table row
- Post-dedup status
- Main text or appendix

No manually transcribed coefficient should enter a table.

### Preferred presentation

- Use coefficient plots for dynamic profiles and heterogeneity.
- Use tables for main estimates, estimand comparisons, and inference.
- Put full lag grids and specification matrices in appendices.
- Put identification assumptions directly beneath the relevant table, not only in methods sections.
- Display economically meaningful baselines and percent-of-mean effects alongside raw units where appropriate.

## 13. Economics-dissertation quality gates

### Gate A — Contribution clarity

For every essay, a reader should be able to answer in one sentence:

- What is the economic question?
- What is the primary estimand?
- What is the identifying variation?
- What is new relative to the literature?

### Gate B — Identification clarity

Every headline result must state:

- Treatment definition
- Comparison group
- Unit and time period
- Fixed effects
- Clustering/inference
- Required identifying assumption
- Main threat and corresponding test

### Gate C — Economic magnitude

Every headline coefficient needs:

- Raw units
- Baseline mean or meaningful denominator
- Confidence interval
- Population to which it applies
- A statement of whether aggregation is defensible

### Gate D — Claim discipline

Audit every abstract, introduction, and conclusion sentence against `Plans/master_evidence_table.md`.

### Gate E — Essay independence

Each essay must have:

- A unique principal question
- A unique lead design or estimand
- A unique main table and figure
- A separate literature contribution
- A conclusion that does not merely restate another essay

### Gate F — Reproducibility

- Every exhibit maps to a script and output.
- All cited county results are post-dedup.
- No stale pre-dedup output is used.
- Build logs record samples and warnings.
- The final pipeline reproduces all headline tables.

## 14. Defense-oriented framing

### Likely question: “Is this really three essays?”

Answer through the estimands:

- Essay 1 estimates incidence.
- Essay 2 estimates adjustment and persistence.
- Essay 3 estimates distribution and institutional observability.

### Likely question: “Is the 2012 event generalizable?”

Answer:

- It is an event-specific ITT.
- The immediate pooled response supports limited generalization.
- The long-run pooled average does not.
- The paper separates those estimands explicitly.

### Likely question: “Where is the health mechanism?”

Answer:

- Medicare provides direct utilization and spending evidence for an older/disabled population.
- It is a separate morbidity channel, not identified mediation of drought-income or working-age debt.

### Likely question: “Why are premiums and debt inconsistent?”

Answer:

- They are institution-specific measures with different populations, geographic pricing rules, and visibility conditions.
- The inconsistency is itself evidence about which costs are recorded and priced.

### Likely question: “Does cold really compound?”

Answer:

- Binned cumulative-dose and long-run event-study estimates support compounding.
- The smooth quadratic specification does not.
- The dissertation reports this estimator dependence rather than concealing it.

### Likely question: “What happened to the structural chapter?”

Answer:

- The approved dissertation replaces it with empirically identified incidence, persistence, and distribution chapters.
- A bounded sufficient-statistics synthesis preserves policy relevance without imposing unsupported structural parameters.

## 15. Definition of a strong finished dissertation

The dissertation is ready for committee circulation when:

- All three essays exist as complete manuscripts.
- Each essay has no more than three headline findings.
- The general introduction states one coherent umbrella contribution.
- The institutional-ledger framing is used consistently.
- The policy section reports transparent scenario bands.
- Every headline number is post-dedup and traceable.
- Employment, debt, premium, and persistence caveats appear beside—not far away from—the associated claims.
- No chapter implies a fully identified morbidity → income → debt → premium chain.
- The appendices contain the extensive robustness work without allowing it to overwhelm the main narrative.
- A skeptical applied economist can identify the estimand, variation, and limitation of every principal result without searching across documents.

## Final strategic rule

> The remaining value comes from selection, explanation, and synthesis—not from adding another regression family.

The empirical repository already meets the substantive threshold for an economics dissertation. The writing should now make a smaller number of results feel inevitable, economically meaningful, and honestly bounded.
